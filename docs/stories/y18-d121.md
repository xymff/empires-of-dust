# A Place at the Fire

*Empires of Dust: Taluma · Year 18 (3983 BCE) · Early Winter, Day 121 · 70 days advanced*

Families open their tables to new introductions, two young adults choose marriage, a market counts its real costs, and a Ridge Hearth learner teaches Taluma on her own terms.

## I. An empty place beside the fire

Seli did not begin by asking who was unmarried. She began by asking which families would welcome guests at their fires. The distinction mattered. Taluma had nineteen unpartnered adults, but some were widowed, some content alone, and some simply uninterested in a new household. No tally could tell the council whose heart was ready.

At the Hall of Many Hands, the families agreed to host small, voluntary meals after work. Parents and relatives could offer introductions, share food and ask questions, but the people being introduced could refuse without embarrassment or losing access to homes, apprenticeships or trade. No children were promised to other households. Visitors from Reedmouth and Shellbend could attend when their own households invited and escorted them, and no one would be counted as a Taluman merely for accepting a meal.

Mavi and Jalem were two already-counted Taluman working-age adults whose names had never needed a separate line in the public chronicle. They had known each other through reed and fish-basket work for years. When relatives suggested sitting together at an evening meal, they accepted. They continued meeting through the next several weeks, sometimes in company and sometimes on the public shore where both felt comfortable.

When Mavi asked Seli whether marriage would require surrendering her apprenticeship, Seli replied, “A household needs more than one pair of hands, but neither pair belongs to the other.” The couple met each other’s relatives and chose a public union near the Hall. They elected to establish their own hearth in an existing safe room, using ordinary unprotected repair materials. They remained the same two residents already included in Taluma’s 112 people.

At the Day 121 reading, there were thirty household hearths, up from twenty-nine, and seventeen unpartnered adults rather than nineteen. Fourteen were working-age and three were elders. The eight grown founding-era children still sharing their families’ hearths were unchanged. Nobody else had been married, moved, or persuaded to leave home. One Shellbend visitor and a Taluman craft learner chose to exchange further invitations, but neither called it an engagement. Their next meeting was theirs to arrange.

## II. The cost written on the back

Nemi had spent years teaching Taluma to count food. Now he asked Dava to count the cost of selling it. The apprentice turned over a clay tally and wrote crew work, boat repairs and preservation fuel on its back. When a visiting buyer held up a sound-looking fish packet, Aret invited the buyer to open a chosen sample rather than trusting Taluma’s marks.

Two households, one from Reedmouth and one from Shellbend, independently accepted another market visit. Crescent Shoal provided a willing craft visitor, not an automatic contract or a spokesman for an entire shore. Taluma sent eleven settlement-days of inspected provisions and received seven days of different food, two prepared poles and two reed bundles. Those materials entered the building ledger, not the food tally. The labor and transport fuel came out of the ordinary resource account rather than being disguised as profit.

Aret’s new shelf comparison again favored the screened, raised method: seventeen of twenty lots were sound at scheduled inspection, versus fourteen of twenty on the older covered shelves. Three settlement-days of damaged or questionable food were discarded this turn. The sample was encouraging, but several windy weeks still lay ahead; nobody offered a guarantee of long-distance shelf life.

At the evening hearing, Iset compared the food sent with the food received. “We lost four food-days on the trade itself,” she said. “And the boats were not free.” Nemi agreed. The returned materials and repeated trust were valuable, and an improved packing sleeve could save future waste. But the council declined guaranteed delivery quantities and asked for one more season of openly audited exchanges before declaring the network reliable.

## III. The visitor who could leave

Renka’s answer arrived through Oru and Tesh: one adult learner from her household, Vela, wanted to visit Taluma’s public workshop. The invitation had been freely offered during an earlier exchange, but no one treated it as a standing right. Vela chose the visit, the host-approved approach and the companions with whom she would travel.

In the Hall, Kelan laid out his carrying frame. Vela inspected the shoulder band and moved the fastening so a loaded carrier could release it without the basket swinging into a leg. Her first demonstration was deliberately made with a light practice weight. The fifth trial shifted when a helper turned too sharply. She stopped the class, asked the learners to describe the failure, and changed the grip rather than declaring success.

Of twelve subsequent level-ground trials with the revised fastening, ten held their loads securely. Two required the carrier to set the load down and reset the straps. Leni credited Vela, Renka, her companion and Kelan on the public teaching record. No steep descent was tested and no path toward Ridge Hearth became permanently open. After two days Vela chose to return with her escorts; Taluma claimed neither her residence nor exclusive ownership of the technique.

Letha and Keshu also reviewed the northern survey plan. Without fresh confirmation of safe tidal conditions and a new Ternhook stopping invitation, no vessel approached the dangerous notch. Instead, younger sailors repeated sounding-line exercises in Taluma’s sheltered water, copying the six older Northreach readings without pretending they described the unknown water beyond the rocks.

On the evening of Mavi and Jalem’s marriage, the guests left an empty space beside the common fire. It was not reserved for a future spouse or a promised child. It was simply a place for someone who might choose to sit there. Taluma was no longer measuring its future only by what it could store or build. It was learning how to invite people into it.
